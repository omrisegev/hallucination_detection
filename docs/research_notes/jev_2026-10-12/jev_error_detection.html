Error detection in Jev and other typed decision models

What Jev is, what work exists on its errors, and whether our label-free step scoring from teacher-forced internals can apply to it.

Short answer

  1. Jev is not a text generator. It is a closed "typed decision model" from TypeSafe AI (released 2026-09-15). You give it a text state and typed questions (Choice, Score, yes/no "Noul"). It returns a probability distribution over the options you defined, in one parallel pass. It writes no reasoning steps.1,2
  2. "No hallucination" means "no out-of-schema output", not "no errors". TypeSafe says so itself: its 0% hallucination number "is not empirical. Schema matching is guaranteed".2 Jev can still choose a valid but wrong option with high probability.
  3. Error detection work on Jev exists, and it is all black-box. About four weeks of preprints and audits measure calibration (ECE), confidence-threshold routing, probability coherence and robustness. Nobody reads internal states, because Jev exposes none. A survey list of the field states that no paper covers probing, hidden states or step-level verification.13
  4. Our method cannot run on Jev itself (no weights, no logprobs, no generated steps). It can run on the open Jev-like models (Kev, Tev1, decider, Strands Decider). Most are Qwen3.5 fine-tunes, close to our Qwen3 setup. This is an open gap.
  5. Jev has been used as a step judge on our benchmark. One community evaluation reports Jev 1.13 at 66.38 "official PRM score" on PRMBench (6,216 items) and 69.5 F1 on ProcessBench.12 Our label-free Qwen3-8B Overall is 65.4 and Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B is 65.5. The protocol is not published, so treat this as a reference point, not a matched comparison.

1. What Jev is

Maker
TypeSafe AI, San Francisco. CEO Diogo Almeida (ex-OpenAI, RLHF / InstructGPT).1
Version
jev-1.13.0, early access since 2026-09-15. 64k context, text only.3
Training
Transformer, synthetic data only, "RL for Calibrated Decisions" (RLCD). No paper, no weights, no method details.1,2
Output
Per-option probabilities. "Confidence" is a function of the same distribution: (pmax − 1/n)/(1 − 1/n) for Choice.4

TypeSafe calls Jev a "System One model": a fast "smart if-statement" inside software, not a reasoner. Multi-step work happens outside the model. The docs tell users to split a decision into separate questions and to combine them in code.2,5 Outside observers suggest Jev may be built on an open-weight LLM, but this is not confirmed.1

The same idea now has an open ecosystem. More than 25 open "Jev-like" models appeared within four weeks. Most are Qwen3.5 / Qwen3.8 fine-tunes with one of three readouts: a pointer head on the hidden state at a <decide> token (Kev), a mask-token encoder head (Laya, ModernBERT), or plain first-token logits over option letters (SemIf, Tev1).13,14,15

Common findings

  • Calibration is good on familiar public tasks and breaks on unseen ones, new languages, and changed answer formats. The sign of the error changes with the question type, so each deployment must fit its own threshold or temperature on 50 to a few hundred labels.8,18
  • The dominant failure is a confident valid wrong answer: no "none of these" option, polar option names, or a label that is not in the text.
  • The only label-free error signal in this literature is probability coherence across related questions.9

3. Does our approach apply?

Our method scores each step of a solution without labels, from teacher-forced internals of an open model (token probabilities and hidden states), and flags the steps most likely to be wrong. Below is how it maps onto three settings.

SettingFitWhy
Jev itself (hosted)noNo weights, no hidden states, no logprobs beyond the option distribution (rounded to 0.01). No generated steps to score. The only signal is the output distribution, which is what RLCD already tunes. Black-box options remain: coherence checks and paraphrase or option-renaming agreement.
Open Jev-like models (Kev, Tev1, decider, Strands Decider, Laya)yes, open gapWeights are open and most are Qwen3.5 fine-tunes. We can read the hidden state at the decision token and the option tokens, and build label-free signals for a wrong decision (for example disagreement between layers, or between the head's probability and the base LM's probability). No published work does this yet.13 The unit is one decision, not a step.
Chains of decisions (workflows, agents such as WebJev)partialA workflow that calls Jev many times is a process: each call is a step. Finding the first wrong call is the same problem as our first-error localization. The "split five ways" result (62.6% → 95% on phishing) shows that decomposition into steps is what makes these models accurate.19 No benchmark with step labels for decision chains exists yet.
Jev as the judge of LLM stepsbaseline / partnerHere Jev competes with us on PRMBench. It is supervised and closed, but very cheap. A fusion of a cheap external judge with our label-free internal score is a possible later study.

Caveat. The repo calls its number an "official PRM score" and has 6,216 records (the full PRMBench size), but it does not show the prompt, how steps are given to Jev, or whether the score uses the same per-category averaging as ours. Do not put this row in the advisor page as a direct comparison without that check. It is safe to say: a closed commercial decision model lands in the same range as our label-free score.

5. Ideas for after the meeting (not now)

Development is frozen until 2026-10-15. These are directions only.

  1. Rerun Jev on PRMBench with our exact protocol and scorer, to get a matched baseline. Cost is about $1.
  2. Apply our label-free internal signals to one open decision model (for example Kev-4B or Tev1-4B, both Qwen3.5) and test if they detect wrong decisions better than its own output probability. Use the CMU judge sets as labels for evaluation only.
  3. Fuse a cheap Jev step verdict with our internal step score on PRMBench, in the same way we fuse scorers today.
