  1. Algorithmic extensions of our unsupervised fusion method, U-PCR
  2. White-box hallucination detection using internal model representations

Below is the main story and what I think we learned. I moved the equations, complete experimental record, confidence intervals and detailed plots into three linked deep dives.

1. Algorithmic extensions of U-PCR

The algorithmic direction grew directly from our discussion about using DUFS more naturally inside U-PCR. In the previous round, I tried DUFS, GroupFS and other selectors as a preprocessing step: they selected the features in advance and then ran L-SML or U-PCR as-is. This time, I replaced U-PCR's own internal keep decision with 111 variants inspired by those feature-selection algorithms. None improved AUROC or moved the selected subset closer to the best label-based subset.

I then did another literature review, focusing specifically on papers that continued the early U-PCR and unsupervised ensemble-regression work. Two became important:

  1. Crowdsourcing Regression: A Spectral Approach (AISTATS 2022) recovers an unknown continuous target from noisy regressors without labels. IU-PCR estimates the shared target direction from covariance under an independence assumption; SU-PCR separates the covariance into a low-rank shared component and sparse correlated regressor errors.
  2. Unsupervised Ensemble Learning Through Deep Energy-based Models (AISTATS 2026) replaces the linear covariance model with an energy-based model over the ensemble outputs and latent class, allowing nonlinear dependencies without labels. Its original categorical input required a new continuous adaptation for our measurements.

I started with IU-PCR and SU-PCR. SU-PCR's sparse-error correction was positive but inconclusive. IU-PCR performed well and gave us a cleaner baseline: it retains the stable measurements and estimates the score through a low-dimensional projected covariance solve.

I then integrated DUFS into this PCR fusion stage. In the earlier experiments DUFS selected features and fusion ran unchanged. In DUFS-LIU-PCR, no feature is simply selected or deleted. Continuous gates define a similarity graph between answers, and its Laplacian enters the final IU-PCR weight problem as a regularizer. Many variants found stable geometry, but it mainly followed shared confidence and response length rather than correctness. IU-PCR and DUFS-LIU-PCR were essentially tied on the aligned 24-cell benchmark.

The most useful change came from HARP. I reused its architectural idea of removing the dominant shared component and examining what remains, not its supervised white-box classifier. I grouped the 30 measurements into six families based on raw provenance: entropy level, entropy change, two energy families, top-probability shape and trace structure.

For each answer, the weighted measurements within a family form one partial score; the six partial scores add exactly to IU-PCR. I regressed each family contribution on the total score and retained its residual—how much more or less the family contributed than expected at the same overall confidence. Family-NRM finds a residual disagreement pattern that repeats across calibration environments and adds it as a small correction. Treating each atomic feature as its own residual direction was unstable and worse. With the six provenance families, PRMBench response AUROC improved from 0.7206 to 0.7252, approximately +0.46 percentage points, with a positive interval.

I then implemented DEEM. The original model receives categorical classifier predictions and learns a nonlinear energy function over the ensemble. Because our inputs are continuous measurements, I first tested hard and rank-based adapters and then developed B3, a graph-free continuous version. B3 preserves the continuous measurements, processes each provenance family through a small nonlinear network, and adds the family contributions into the final score.

The first extensions focused on B3's output: sample-dependent family routers and residual or graph-based corrections. Although these mechanisms were active, they did not consistently beat static or permuted controls. As with DUFS-LIU, unsupervised dependence did not tell us which measurement was more correct for a particular answer.

I therefore moved the intervention to the input. A dependency audit showed that the historical families were strongly correlated and that B3 mostly preserved their shared variation. I organized nine core measurements as a 3-by-3 source-by-operator structure: entropy, sampled-token surprisal and partition energy, each measured by mean, sliding variance and CUSUM. For each coordinate, CIW uses five-fold cross-fitting to predict its shared component from the four coordinates with the same source or operator. The residual is its innovation. The original measurement and standardized innovation are mixed using a fixed, label-free weight derived from out-of-fold predictability: predictable coordinates receive more innovation correction, unpredictable coordinates remain near the original value, and at least half of the original measurement is retained. The unchanged B3 model is then fitted on these transformed inputs.

This produced CIW-DEEM—Cross-fitted Innovation-Weighted DEEM. It uses dependence conservatively to separate shared signal from feature-specific innovation, rather than interpreting dependence as correctness. It reaches 0.7492 equal-dataset-family AUROC and 0.7820 cell-macro AUROC on the aligned 24-cell benchmark. Its +0.000732 equal-family gain over B3—approximately +0.073 AUROC points—is the highest point estimate among comparable B3 input variants, but the sign-flip test is inconclusive and the gain is below the +0.25-point promotion threshold. It therefore remains the leading challenger from this line, not a promoted replacement; the small benefit also appears specific to nonlinear B3 rather than a generic improvement to IU-PCR, DUFS-LIU or linear prediction.

There were many unsuccessful attempts between these steps. They remain in the deep dive; the sequence above is the main route from feature selection to IU/SU-PCR, DUFS-LIU-PCR, Family-NRM and CIW-DEEM.

2. White-box direction — internal layers add coverage and signal, but not yet a validated win

The main reference here was TriLens: Per-Layer Logit-Lens Entropy for White-Box Hallucination Detection. TriLens reads the attention output, MLP output and residual stream at every transformer layer and trains a supervised probe. I collected a related but richer internal representation on 14 dataset-model cells across nine model families: per-layer entropy, target-token NLL, top-1 surprisal, target gap, entropy excess and divergence from the final layer.

The question was whether our label-free fusion machinery could combine these depth trajectories without training a supervised classifier. I tested U-PCR, DUFS-LIU, hierarchical depth groups and residual-family fusion. The initial compact representation did not beat final-layer NLL. A richer distributed-depth representation was much stronger and reached about 0.785 macro AUROC, above our local supervised TriLens-style approximation.

On the exact 31,440 answers where both final methods are available, the aggregate result is a practical tie: 0.7817 for pure white-box U-PCR versus 0.7830 for gray-box DUFS-LIU. The paired interval includes zero. White-box does provide broader coverage—42,238 scorable answers versus 31,467 complete gray-box answers. A simple white+gray average reaches 0.7902, suggesting complementarity, but it was proposed after seeing these data and is not promoted.

My interpretation: internal layers contain useful complementary information and solve a real coverage problem, but an independent preregistered run is still needed before claiming that white-box access improves detection.

3. Application-oriented extensions beyond final-answer detection

I tested whether the same uncertainty measurements and fusion principles remain useful on four distinct tasks: first-error localization, fixed-prefix prediction, actual stopping and RAG hallucination detection. Each task is evaluated separately against its relevant benchmark and comparators.

A. First-error localization — a consistent improvement over Mind the Gap

ProcessBench differs from final-answer detection: the system must identify the first erroneous reasoning step, or return no error. Our GL-LIU pipeline uses a global IU/DUFS-LIU head to detect an error and a local token-level head to locate it. We compare it with our reproduction of Mind the Gap under the same protocol.

More generally, I reconstructed all 13 answer-level fusion methods as alternative response heads and paired each with the same token head. The response head summarizes whether the complete trace looks unreliable. The token head fuses 29 uncertainty trajectories with IU-PCR, scores every token and assigns each reasoning step its maximum token risk. ProcessBench combines the response and step ranks before selecting a first error or no error; PRMBench labels every step and can use the token score directly.

Most localization ability comes from token-level fusion. The response head adds a smaller but useful ProcessBench improvement because that task also needs a clean-trace decision. On PRMBench, adding global response risk hurts step ranking.

Separate PRMBench experiment. PRMBench labels every step, not only the first error. The token-only trajectory-first IU-PCR reaches 0.6712 AUROC; supervised Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B reaches 0.7983 and remains a higher-access ceiling.

Later CIW-DEEM transfer. CIW replaced only the response head while the token-IU29 locator remained fixed. It reached 30.91% ProcessBench macro F1 versus 31.02% for B3 with the same token head, and 0.5811 versus 0.5842 on PRMBench. This did not improve localization, and it did not test CIW at token level. The useful follow-up is a token-native innovation model.

B. Fixed-prefix prediction — a clear gain at both tested budgets

We can predict final-answer correctness meaningfully before the answer is complete, and the selected prefix model clearly improves over our earlier shared causal baseline.

That baseline, Unified-28, compresses seven token-level uncertainty streams into four causal summaries per stream—the current level, a short moving average, accumulated positive evidence and persistence—giving 28 features in total. One frozen IU-PCR weight vector produces a risk score at every prefix, using only information already available at that token.

The selected model uses a more task-specific two-head architecture. Its global head recomputes the answer-level IU uncertainty score using only the observed prefix; its local head follows the strongest token-level warning seen so far. After calibration, the two scores are combined with equal weight. This preserves both the overall state of the incomplete answer and a sharp local warning that might otherwise be averaged away. DeepConf motivated the confidence-under-compute question, although our single-trace method is an adaptation rather than a paper-exact reproduction.

On the aligned ProcessBench prefix benchmark, the two-head model reaches 0.5955 AUROC at 64 tokens versus 0.5629 for Unified-28, and 0.6572 at 256 tokens versus 0.6114; both paired intervals are above zero. This supports useful prediction from fixed saved prefixes, not yet adaptive stopping during live generation.

C. Actual stopping — substantial compute savings, but not a deployment win

The stopping result is negative as a matched-accuracy claim. Unlike fixed-prefix prediction, this policy must decide online when to terminate the model's reasoning.

LEASH is a separate stopping policy rather than another U-PCR variant. At every token it monitors entropy, the gap between the two most likely tokens and the maximum token probability. After a minimum reasoning period, it stops when uncertainty has fallen and reached a plateau while the confidence margin is no longer changing substantially. It then forces a short greedy closure so that the model still produces a final answer. I compared this actually stopped generation with unrestricted chain-of-thought generation on the same questions.

Across six eligible model-dataset cells on AQuA and GSM8K, LEASH reduces generated tokens by 38.8%, but pass@1 falls by 18.3 percentage points and decreases in every cell. Two Mistral cells are protocol-blocked because their tokenizer lacks the required chat template. This is a real accuracy–compute frontier, not matched-accuracy stopping.

D. RAG hallucination detection — useful transfer, but strongly task-dependent

The RAG result is mixed rather than one general win. For the same generated answer, I rescore every token with the retrieved context, without the context and—when available—with one retrieved document removed at a time. Changes in token probability, entropy and distribution shape measure how strongly each claim depends on its evidence. A fixed label-free IU-PCR head fuses these evidence-contrast features into token risk; the scores are then averaged over a sentence or the full answer when the benchmark requires a sentence- or answer-level prediction.

I kept seven panels separate because their prediction units and access assumptions differ. On RAGTruth, our fixed RAG-IU method reaches 0.7274/0.6892/0.6587 AUROC at answer/sentence/token level, with substantial QA versus Data-to-Text heterogeneity. On the local GASP sentence benchmark, I also tested a simpler score that standardizes and combines four evidence-sensitivity measurements: loss and distribution change without context, and their largest change when one document is removed. This GASP-style score is 0.6708 versus 0.6597 for matched fixed IU, with an interval crossing zero. LettuceDetect is not another version of our method; it is a separate supervised example-level ceiling at 0.7929 F1. On RefChecker, fixed IU is applied to fixed, pre-extracted claims and reaches 0.6645/0.6402/0.7506 AUROC under accurate/noisy/zero context, which remain separate settings. The fusion principle transfers, but these tasks cannot be pooled into one RAG headline.

My interpretation: the application work contains two positive results—consistent improvement over Mind the Gap in localization and a clear fixed-prefix gain. LEASH exposes an unfavorable stopping frontier, while RAG transfer remains task-dependent.

I now see the three directions as one connected thesis story. The algorithmic work explains why stable dependence is not necessarily target-aligned; the white-box work asks whether new internal measurements provide the missing information; and the applications show where the same uncertainty machinery is more useful when the target is localization or early prediction rather than only completed-answer ranking.

For the next stage, I would like to discuss which prospective confirmation should come first: token-native CIW/innovation, independent white-box validation, or new-data localization and prefix prediction. My current preference is to prioritize the two application directions that already show positive evidence—first-error localization and fixed-prefix prediction—while developing token-native innovation fusion as their algorithmic follow-up. CIW-DEEM at answer level and the white+gray combination remain frozen challengers.
