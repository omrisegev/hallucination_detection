Experiment 3 - Is there a right number of measurements?

Whether accuracy peaks at a middle size, and whether the fit score can find good combinations. Recomputed on the current pool.

In short

  • The 1.03M-combination cache on disk is unusable - only 5/19 test sets still reproduce (worst disagreement 0.37 AUROC).
  • No sweet spot. For a typical combination bigger is better in 25/25 test sets, so a 'stop where the curve turns' rule has nothing to find.
  • But the best combination at each size sits ~4.1 points above a typical one: trimming has a high ceiling and a poor average. The value is entirely in choosing well.
  • The fit score does carry signal - pointing the wrong way. Correlation with accuracy is +0.223, positive in 24/25 test sets, meaning combinations that fit the one-cause model WORSE are MORE accurate. The algorithm minimises misfit, so it steers against the gradient.
  • An earlier pass reported this correlation as near zero; that pass used the stale cache and is superseded by the number above.

  • No middle peak. For a typical combination, keeping more is better in 25/25 test sets, and the best typical size is 14 or more in 22/25.
  • But the best combinations found are small — 8 measurements or fewer in 16/25 test sets — and the upper line runs about 4.1 points above the lower one.
  • Together: trimming has a high ceiling and a poor average. Cutting measurements at random makes things worse; cutting the right ones makes things much better. All the value is in choosing well, none in being small.
  • Consequence for the algorithm. A rule of the form "trim while the curve improves, stop at the turn" has no turn to find. Size must be fixed in advance or set by an error-control threshold — it cannot be discovered by following this curve.

Question 2 - Does the fit score know which combinations are good?

It does - but with the sign reversed from what the algorithm assumes. Mean correlation +0.223 (median +0.185), and positive in 24 of 25 test sets. The fit score is "misfit": lower means the one-hidden-cause model explains the measurements better. A positive correlation with accuracy therefore means combinations that fit the model worse are more accurate.
  • The trimming algorithm removes whichever measurement most improves the fit. On this evidence that is steering directly against the gradient — it optimises for the combinations that score lower.
  • This is the same finding as Experiment 2, seen from another angle. There, repairing the worst-fitting group was worse than repairing a random one. Here, worse-fitting combinations score higher. Both say: in this data, poor fit to a single-cause model marks where the signal is. The strongest measurements are near-duplicates of one another, and that duplication is exactly what a one-factor model cannot absorb.
  • The actionable version is that the criterion is not useless — it is inverted. A rule that preserves badly-fitting structure, or that trims to increase misfit, is the version worth testing next. That is a different algorithm from the one tested here, and it has not been run.
  • Caveat on strength. A correlation of +0.22 is moderate, not strong. It is enough to say the sign is real and consistent (24/25); it is not enough to expect a large gain from acting on it alone.
Why an earlier reading of this said the opposite. A first pass used the cached sweep and reported a correlation near zero with an inconsistent sign. That pass was reading the stale cache audited in Part 1 - 5/19 of whose test sets no longer reproduce. The number above is the one computed on current data, and it supersedes it.
