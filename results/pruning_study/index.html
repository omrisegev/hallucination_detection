Trimming study - can we pick measurements without answer keys?

Five experiments on 25 test sets. Every number recomputed on current data; every chart has its raw data beside it.

In short

  • The fit score the trimming algorithm steers by has its sign backwards. Combinations that fit the one-cause model worse are more accurate (+0.22, 24/25 test sets), and repairing the worst-fitting group is 2.2 points worse than repairing a random one.
  • Why: the strongest measurements are near-duplicates of each other, and that duplication is what makes the fit look bad. Poor fit marks where the signal is, not where the junk is.
  • Trimming has a high ceiling and a poor average - a typical smaller set is worse than keeping everything (25/25), but the best small sets are much better. All the value is in choosing well.
  • Nothing tested closes the weight-estimation gap. All 16 weighting pipelines land within 1.2 points of each other and below the six hand-picked measurements.
  • About a million cached scored combinations turned out to be stale (only 5/19 test sets still reproduce) - everything here was recomputed.

What this study is testing

One question, in two halves:

Can a score computed without any answer keys decide both which measurements to trust and how many to keep, well enough to match a set that was hand-picked using answer keys?

The detector takes about 30 different measurements from a language model while it writes an answer, and combines them into one number: how likely the answer is wrong. Two things in that pipeline are open — which measurements to keep, and how much to trust each one. This study attacks both.

Which bar matters. An automatic picker chooses its measurements itself, per test set, with no answer keys — the best one is a6.pl_dufs at 0.7524, and that is the bar any new automatic method has to clear. A fixed subset was chosen once using answer keys and then reused: GOOD_6 (0.7594) and LOCO_5 (0.7705, on 24 of 25 test sets) are both of that kind. GOOD_6 is used throughout as the anti-regression anchor — every experiment must reproduce it — but it is not a fair target for a label-free method.

The gap that matters: the same 30 measurements with the same directions score 0.7664 when averaged equally and 0.7809 when a model learns the trust levels from answer keys. That 1.45-point difference is the entire value of knowing how much to trust each measurement, and it is what the weighting experiments are trying to recover without labels.

What the five experiments add up to

Two findings recur across experiments and are the substance of this study.

1. The fit score is informative, but its sign is backwards

The trimming algorithm steers by how well a "everything here is a noisy reading of one hidden thing" model explains the measurements, and removes whatever most improves that fit. Two independent experiments say that is the wrong direction:

  • Experiment 3: across combinations of equal size, those that fit the model worse are more accurate (correlation +0.22, positive in 24 of 25 test sets).
  • Experiment 2: repairing the worst-fitting group is worse than repairing a randomly chosen one, by 2.2 points.

The explanation is the same in both cases. The strongest measurements — average uncertainty per token, average surprise per token, confidence in the chosen word — are near-duplicates of each other. That duplication is exactly the extra shared structure a single-factor model cannot absorb, so it shows up as bad fit. In this data, poor fit marks where the signal is concentrated, not where the junk is. Any rule that trims toward better fit is removing the informative measurements first.

2. Trimming has a high ceiling and a poor average

A typical smaller combination is worse than keeping everything, in 25 of 25 test sets. But the best combination found at each size sits several points above the typical one, and the best ones found are small. So the entire value of trimming lives in choosing the right measurements — there is no benefit to being small as such, and no middle size that is good on average. A stopping rule of the form "trim while the curve improves, stop at the turn" has no turn to find.

The experiments

Experiment 1 - Does the grouping step help?

Switching off the detector's internal 'group similar measurements' stage, at every subset size.

At all 30 measurements, switching grouping off changes accuracy by +1.19 points (5/6 test sets better without it).

Experiment 2 - Trimming by fixing the worst-fitting group

Omri's cluster-localized algorithm, run for the first time, with controls for the group choice and the tie-breaker.

Best arm: Localize to a RANDOM group (control) at 0.7302. The previously recorded 0.7004 for this idea is void - that prototype's fit score was zero by construction.

Experiment 3 - Is there a right number of measurements?

Whether accuracy peaks at a middle size, and whether the fit score can find good combinations.

No middle peak: bigger is better for a typical combination in 25/25 test sets, so there is no turn for a stopping rule to find. The fit score correlates +0.223 with accuracy (positive in 24/25) - meaning worse-fitting combinations score higher, the opposite of what the algorithm assumes. The 1.03M-combination cache on disk is unusable - only 5/19 test sets still reproduce.

Experiment 4 - Where the label-free weights go wrong

Diagnosing the 1.45-point gap between guessed and learned trust levels.

A second hidden factor sits at 0.28 of the first, so the one-cause premise is only approximate. Guessed vs learned trust levels: rank agreement +0.186, top-5 overlap 1/5.

Experiment 5 - How to weight the measurements

Sixteen weighting pipelines as a three-slot factorial.

All 16 weighting pipelines land between 0.7434 and 0.7555 - a 1.2-point spread, all below the six hand-picked measurements (0.7594).

How results are reported

  • No result is claimed on a small difference in accuracy. With 25 test sets, differences below about a point are not distinguishable from sampling noise, so win/loss counts and significance tests are reported alongside every average and nothing is adopted on an average alone.
  • Every experiment re-checks the anchor. The six hand-picked measurements must reproduce 0.7594 before any number is reported; if they do not, the data being loaded is not the data our existing numbers came from.
  • Every chart has its raw numbers beside it as CSV, so any figure can be re-derived, re-plotted or re-tested later.

A note on speed

Two functions in the shared fusion code were doing avoidable work: a matrix that costs O(m4) to build was being computed even when nothing read it, and two inner loops were written in Python. Fixing all three left the output bit-identical and made the combining step about 100 times faster at 30 measurements, which is what made these experiments affordable at all.
