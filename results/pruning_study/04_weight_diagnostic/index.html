Experiment 4 - Where the label-free weights go wrong

Diagnosing the 1.45-point gap between guessed and learned trust levels, before attempting any repair.

In short

  • The one-hidden-cause premise is only approximate: a second factor sits at 0.28 of the first, and one factor explains a median 82% of the structure.
  • Every test set contains near-duplicate measurements (largest correlation 0.99-1.00), which is exactly what breaks the weighting recipe.
  • Guessed vs learned trust levels: rank agreement +0.186, sign agreement 0.57, top-5 overlap 1/5.
  • No winner is declared here - this experiment exists to aim the repair, not to pick one.

The gap being diagnosed

Using the same 30 measurements and the same directions, a model trained on answer keys scores 0.7809 while simply averaging them scores 0.7664. That 1.45 point difference is purely the value of knowing how much to trust each measurement. Our detector has to guess those trust levels from structure alone. This experiment measures how the guess fails.

1. Does the "one hidden cause" picture hold?

The whole weighting recipe assumes every measurement is a noisy reading of a single hidden thing — whether the answer is right. If that were exactly true, the relationships between measurements would be explainable by one factor and a second factor would be absent.

  • Median strength of a second factor relative to the first: 0.281. A single factor explains a median of 82% of the structure.
  • The premise is only approximately true. There is real structure the one-factor picture does not capture — which is exactly the room a better estimator would have to exploit.

2. How redundant are the measurements?

Every test set contains near-duplicate measurements — largest correlation between 0.996 and 1.000, with 24–105 pairs above 0.75. Near-duplicates are precisely what breaks the assumption the weighting recipe needs, and they are also what Experiment 2's localizer finds sitting in the worst-fitting group.

  • Rank agreement (does the guess order measurements the same way?): median +0.186.
  • Sign agreement (does it get each measurement's direction right?): median 0.57. This is measured after resolving the single global flip that the combined score carries anyway — charging that one bit to all 30 measurements would have understated agreement.
  • Top-5 overlap (do they agree on which few matter most?): median 1/5.
  • Weight concentration: the top three measurements carry a median 17% of the total guessed weight.

What this points at

The failure is not one thing. Read the three columns together:

  • Sign agreement near 0.57 means direction errors are a substantial part of the problem.
  • Rank agreement of +0.186 means the ordering is largely wrong — the guess and the learned model do not agree about which measurements matter.
  • A second factor at 0.28 of the first means repairs that assume a single clean factor are working against the data, and the honest options are either to model a second factor or to remove the measurements that create it.

This experiment deliberately produces no winner and no gate. Its only job is to say which repair is worth attempting.
